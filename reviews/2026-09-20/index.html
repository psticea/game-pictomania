Pictomania:
keep the board, strengthen the game.

A detailed UI, usability, and Romanian content review. The visual direction is already distinctive; the next gains are safer rounds, dependable reading, and better-curated cards.

The verdict

Do not redesign this into a conventional game dashboard. The three-column signal board is the right idea for people drawing and glancing up from a table. Keep its speed, its large words, and its redundant letter/color system. Make the active round harder to lose, the mobile compromises explicit, and the card combinations fairer.

The biggest problem is not that the site looks unfinished. It is that the interface presents a confident, stable board while the underlying experience is fragile: one key can replace it, reload loses it, duplicated prompts can appear across columns, and unsupported device features fail silently.

Product lens: a Romanian word-board companion for 3–6 friends using physical Pictomania components, with one tablet or laptop read from roughly a metre away. This is an operate surface, not a marketing page. Recommendations optimize uninterrupted play rather than sign-ups, decorative features, or more chrome.

What is already working

The design is specific to the job. Its scoreboard-like composition, colored coordinates, condensed display type, and lack of navigation suit shared-table play. It does not look like an interchangeable SaaS template.

  • The complete board is immediately available. There is no account, modal, setup wizard, loading shell, or screen transition before seeing the 21 words. Local loading and all ordinary core actions worked.
  • Column identity is strong. A/B/C plus amber/cyan/magenta is much safer than color alone. Repeating the row numbers in each column reduces eye travel. Keep both forms of identification.
  • Text contrast is excellent on the main reading surface. Measured white-on-base contrast was approximately 16.29:1. Large letter contrast on the solid headers ranged from 7.55:1 to 10.36:1. These are targeted measurements, not a full accessibility certification.
  • The implementation is appropriately small. The five app assets total 32,766 bytes before fonts and HTTP overhead. No framework or build tool is necessary. Do not add one just to make these improvements.
  • Useful accessibility foundations exist. Real buttons, Romanian document language, named column regions, ordered lists, selected-state announcements, a polite status region, visible focus styles, and a reduced-motion alternative are already present.

Detailed findings

P1 Protect the active round before adding features

Observed: the all-card button, per-column buttons, difficulty selectors, and the global R/1/2/3 keys immediately change the board. None can be undone. A reload kept the selected difficulties but replaced all three cards. Only pictomania.diff is persisted.

Why it matters: the board is shared reference material, not disposable search results. A stray key or touch can make everyone's assigned word disappear in the middle of drawing. The UI has no concept of “we are playing with these cards now.”

Recommendation: first add one-step restoration for every board-changing action, including difficulty changes, and save the current board using stable card IDs. Then introduce a lightweight “Blochează tabla” action: while locked, disable dealing and difficulty changes, including their shortcuts. Keep setup fast; do not show a confirmation dialog on every normal deal.

A compact preparation/play distinction would also let management controls recede during a round without removing coordinate labels. “New round” should be an intentional action; reloading should restore, not silently redeal.

Done when: a mistaken single-card, all-card, or difficulty change can restore the exact previous 21 words and difficulty states; reload restores the active board; a locked round cannot be changed by pointer or keyboard.

Evidence: app.js:10–19, 47–62, 81–88, 123–140, 168–182. Browser reload and shortcut flows reproduced the behavior. Confidence: high.

P1 Enforce distinct prompts, not just distinct themes

Observed: pickIndex() excludes the other columns' theme names but never compares their words. “Animale de companie” and “Animale mici de casă” are allowed together despite sharing Hamster, Broască țestoasă, Iepure, and Peștișor.

Why it matters: a duplicated prompt makes a drawing less informative about the intended coordinate and can create avoidable confusion. This is a content-selection problem with a direct effect on the fairness and clarity of play, not merely database tidiness.

Recommendation: choose a compatible set of three cards as a unit. Reject exact normalized word collisions across the visible board. Define an editorial policy for synonyms and ambiguous homonyms separately; for example, “Rachetă” can mean different things in different categories. Keep each card's themed seven-word structure rather than randomly mixing unrelated words to remove collisions.

Done when: every dealt board contains 21 distinct normalized prompts; a single-column replacement remains compatible with the other two; an exhausted eligible pool produces an explicit, recoverable choice rather than silently breaking the rule.

Evidence: app.js:23–44; data.js, the two animal cards. Seeded 10,000-board simulations using the actual selector found collisions on 672 all-easy boards, 277 all-medium boards, and 414 mixed easy/medium/easy boards. These are synthetic samples, not measured real-user incidence. Confidence: high.

P1 Treat reading distance as a requirement, not just screen fit

Observed: with the intended web fonts, all 100 cards fitted within their rows across seven tested viewport sizes. That is a real strength. However, at 390 × 844, the words are 19.11px and indices 11.47px; at 320 × 568, they are 15.68px and 9.41px. Several long words split mid-word in the narrow columns.

Why it matters: your own product brief says players should not lean in from roughly a metre away. The row number is half of the coordinate, not incidental metadata. Tiny indices and broken words undermine rapid “C5” lookup even when no element technically overflows.

Recommendation: keep the three simultaneous columns on the shared board; do not stack them into a long scrolling page. Give indices a stronger minimum size and a more consistent gutter. Provide an optional table-display setting that reduces setup chrome and allocates more space to the board. On small phones, communicate that this is a close-up view and recommend a larger shared screen.

Do not promise that rotation solves everything: at 844 × 390 the measured word size is still 19.11px. Landscape improves line length in this layout, not necessarily the viewing distance. Compare uppercase Oswald against a slightly less condensed or mixed-case option in a short reading test before replacing the established typography.

Done when: all 21 coordinates and prompts remain visible with the longest cards; numbers are easy to acquire; real players can locate random coordinates on the intended tablet/laptop from the intended distance without leaning in. Set the timing target from that test, not from a guessed universal pixel threshold.

Evidence: styles.css:35–36, 307–335, 388–425; full-deck geometry sweep. Physical distance, glare, and viewing-angle performance were not tested. Confidence: high for dimensions; medium for the real-world effect pending a table test.

P2 Preserve category context and explain the companion role

Observed: theme names disappear at widths of 720px and below, and in short landscape layouts. The page contains no brief explanation of what the board replaces or how coordinates are used. The only instructional copy is the keyboard hint, which itself disappears below 900px and is hidden from assistive technology.

Why it matters: category names help disambiguate words such as “Bancă” and “Rachetă”. Hiding them changes the information available to phone users. A newcomer can also reasonably mistake this for a complete digital game and look for word assignment, drawing, or scoring tools that are not part of the app.

Recommendation: retain a short theme label where it fits, or make full category text easy to reveal without changing a card. Add a small “Cum folosești tabla” help disclosure, not a mandatory tutorial or permanent paragraph over the game. Explain that it is a word board used alongside the game, and show one coordinate example.

Done when: a first-time visitor can identify the site's limited role, find a requested coordinate, and discover each card's theme at every supported size. Existing players can ignore the help entirely.

Evidence: index.html:19, 31–50; styles.css:267–277, 367–381, 419–425. Confidence: high.

P2 Make controls comfortable to hit and unambiguous to navigate

Observed: desktop difficulty buttons are 38px high; the card-deal button is 44 × 38px. Phone portrait difficulty buttons are 32px high and the deal button 38 × 34px. Landscape shrinks some controls to 30px high. These fall short of the project's own 44px target, although that alone is not proof of failure of WCAG's 24px minimum-target criterion.

Keyboard: all 11 controls are reachable with Tab, and focus is strong against the dark bar. But the shared light-cyan focus color measures only approximately 1.33:1, 1.45:1, and 1.82:1 against the three solid colored headers. Strengthen it there with a dark or two-tone outline and verify the actual focused control.

Names and shortcuts: the three deal buttons all announce “Carte nouă”; their containing regions provide context, but a screen-reader button list loses that context. The difficulty groups have no accessible names. Global single-character shortcuts cannot be disabled or remapped and are not limited to a focused control, creating a WCAG 2.1.4 concern as well as an accidental-action risk.

Recommendation: use at least 44 × 44px hit areas where the product promises them. Name controls “Carte nouă pentru A” and groups “Dificultate pentru cartea A”. Offer shortcut settings or focus-scoped activation, expose instructions accessibly, and prevent key-repeat from dealing many cards when a key is held.

Done when: controls are comfortable on a real tablet, focus remains clearly visible on every header, accessible names identify the target column, and single-letter shortcuts can be disabled, remapped, or appropriately focus-scoped.

Evidence: index.html:19, 39–45; app.js:66–93, 174–182; styles.css:62–65, 232–264, 380–425. Contrast values use sRGB conversion of computed solid colors; striped and anti-aliased pixels were not fully audited. Confidence: high.

P2 Make difficulty mean something consistent

Observed: “Mediu” ranges from concrete animals and recognizable objects to Nostalgie, Telepatie, Documentar, and Kinetoterapeut. Meanwhile, the easy deck contains near-neighbor berries and specialized percussion instruments. Recognizing the word, knowing what it looks like, and distinguishing it from the other six are three different challenges.

Why it matters: the difficulty selector promises a predictable experience. Right now, selecting medium can change the kind of challenge as much as its intensity. Some groups will experience this as arbitrary rather than funny or competitive.

Recommendation: curate each card on vocabulary familiarity, drawability, and distinguishability within its seven-word set. Keep two visible difficulty choices initially; do not expand the interface into a large taxonomy. Move or rewrite problematic cards after a small playtest, and make abstract/specialist packs opt-in only if demand justifies them.

Done when: each card has an editorial rationale for its level, and test groups report a reasonably consistent challenge within that level. Unknown-word frequency and “impossible to distinguish” complaints should be recorded separately.

Evidence: all 100 cards in data.js, especially “Fructe de pădure,” “Instrumente de percuție,” “Stări sufletești,” “Planete,” “Personaje SF,” and “Profesii din spital.” Difficulty judgments are editorial hypotheses until playtested. Confidence: medium.

P2 Optimize for fresh sessions, not endless random clicking

Observed: there are exactly 50 easy and 50 medium cards, with fixed word order. The picker avoids the same card in the same slot immediately, but maintains no session history. A card can return soon, and a theme from the previous board can move into a different column during an all-card deal.

Why it matters: extending replayability is the product's core purpose. A continually refreshable board is not an infinite content supply. With three easy cards per round, even perfect no-repeat scheduling must reuse a card by round 17; the current independent random draws can repeat much earlier.

Recommendation: use a session-aware shuffled queue per difficulty, while still enforcing cross-column compatibility. Make “new cards” a board-level transaction that excludes all three previous cards when possible. Defer conflicting cards rather than consuming them invisibly. Add a restrained progress/exhaustion message in setup, not a permanent game-screen statistic.

Only consider randomizing row order between rounds after playtesting and confirming the intended game variant. It must never change during an active round; it is not a substitute for better content.

Done when: recent cards do not return unexpectedly, eligible deck exhaustion is explicit, and the user can intentionally reset a session. Difficulty changes do not silently reinterpret an old numeric card index as a different card's identity.

Evidence: app.js:16, 33–44, 83–87, 138–140; fixed arrays in data.js. In the seeded all-easy simulation, 618 of 10,000 redeals retained a prior-board theme somewhere; that is not the same as repeating it in the same slot. Confidence: high.

P2 Tell the host whether fullscreen and wake prevention worked

Observed: fullscreen and screen wake lock succeeded in the tested Chromium session. Their rejection paths, however, deliberately ignore errors. Simulated denial produced no visible message or status text. The fullscreen button still says “Ecran complet” after entering fullscreen.

Why it matters: “the shared board stays awake” is a core promise. Silent failure makes the host discover a device limitation only when the screen dims or the button appears broken.

Recommendation: reflect the actual fullscreen state with an exit label and matching icon. Show a small, non-blocking status only when necessary: wake prevention active, unavailable, or released. Handle unsupported APIs and denied permissions differently where there is useful recovery guidance. Avoid turning a low-risk limitation into a modal interruption.

Done when: rejected or unsupported APIs produce an accurate message; the label follows fullscreen entry/exit, including Escape; returning to the tab updates wake status without accumulating redundant requests.

Evidence: app.js:145–164, 185; index.html:24. API denial was injected in an isolated browser context, not a natural failure on the user's device. Safari/iOS behavior was not tested. Confidence: high for silent handling; device coverage remains open.

P2 Make offline behavior and font fallback genuinely dependable

Observed: dealing continues if the already-loaded page loses its connection. A reload while offline failed in the fresh test context. There is no service worker or offline application cache strategy. The visual system also requests Oswald and DM Sans from Google Fonts.

Failure case: blocking the font hosts at 320 × 568 caused “Broască țestoasă” to occupy approximately 72.75px vertically in a 59.94px row. The intended-font sweep had no such overrun. Fallback type therefore affects more than appearance; it can cross row boundaries.

Recommendation: self-host the necessary font files with their license notices, and select a fallback whose metrics are deliberately tested. If opening/reloading offline is a requirement, cache the complete small app shell, fonts, and data with a versioned update policy. Keep the active board stable when an update arrives; offer updates between rounds.

Done when: the documented offline promise matches behavior; a previously prepared installation can open offline if that is the promise; blocked or delayed fonts cannot cause word overlap; an update never replaces an active round.

Evidence: index.html:9–11; styles.css:43–53, 319–335; offline reload and font-host failure experiments. A locally saved complete folder is a separate distribution model from a cached hosted website. Confidence: high.

P3 Refine the hierarchy without diluting the identity

Observed: the colored headers already establish a strong hierarchy, but the diagonal medium-difficulty pattern covers the full header, including the letter/category area. It competes with the stable column color. Setup controls remain visible throughout play, while category labels are tiny even on large screens.

Recommendation: retain the saturated column colors and bold word typography. Confine the medium pattern to a small redundant difficulty marker or a quieter header zone, keeping the selected “Mediu” label explicit. Make category names easier to read in setup. If active-round locking is introduced, use that state to reduce control emphasis rather than adding another toolbar.

Preserve the alternating row bands: they help tracking. Tune separator visibility on the actual display rather than increasing contrast indiscriminately. Test whether a slightly more prominent number gutter improves coordinate lookup more than making every word larger.

Done when: players identify the column and row before noticing setup controls, while hosts can still change settings quickly between rounds. Compare variants in the same lighting and at the same viewing distance.

Evidence: desktop and tablet screenshots; styles.css:198–207, 267–277, 291–335. This is design judgment, not a measured task-performance result. Confidence: medium.

The player experience

Cognitive load: simple overall, fragile at the wrong moments

The 3 × 7 structure is intrinsic to the task. Do not reduce it to four items just to follow a generic UX rule. All 21 choices need to coexist so players can compare drawings against the board. Spatial grouping and repeated coordinates already do most of the work.

The unnecessary load comes from managing an active round: remembering what was there before an accident, inferring hidden themes, interpreting abstract prompts, and deciding whether the screen will stay awake. Eleven visible buttons are not automatically too many; keeping all the board-changing buttons active during drawing is the more consequential issue.

PlayerJourneyLikely friction
Experienced hostOpens the board, adjusts difficulty, deals, then reaches across the table between rounds.Fast setup works. Small targets, no round lock, and no undo make a mistaken reach costly.
First-time guestLooks for B4, then tries to interpret the requested word.No coordinate example or scope explanation; hidden phone themes make ambiguous words harder to interpret.
Player reading from farther awayGlances up while drawing, using color, letter, and number to find a row.Strong desktop contrast helps. Small mobile numbers and split words slow recognition; actual physical testing is still needed.
Keyboard or assistive-technology userTabs through controls or opens a list of buttons.Real controls and regions help, but repeated button names, weak header focus, and global character shortcuts introduce ambiguity.

Emotional arc: the initial board feels immediate and confident; the best moment is quickly spotting the right word; the worst moments are an unexplained duplicate, an accidental redraw, or losing the screen mid-round. Prioritize those valleys before adding celebratory animations.

Do not prioritize yet: accounts, online multiplayer, a drawing canvas, scoreboards, sounds, a timer, achievements, chat, or a large navigation system. Three decisions worth settling before implementation: which physical screen is the primary shared display; whether “offline” includes closing and reopening the site; and how strict the app should be about synonyms versus identical word strings. Method, provenance, and limitations

The single-context notice refers only to the design skill's independent-review protocol, not to whether the website was actually run. Source inspection, real browser interactions, geometry checks, and the automated design detector were performed.

Version: the original local checkout contained only the initial README. Following the clarification to review the latest local/remote main containing 4a1fa36, local main was fast-forwarded to 59a2e1b43325faa533a4da5e9d9ce33149d843ac. Remote main matched that SHA when checked. No application code, content data, styles, or project settings were edited.

Read: PRODUCT.md, README.md, all of index.html, styles.css, app.js, and all 100 cards in data.js. The product brief, not a generic visual template, supplied the design criteria.

Run: a loopback-only local static server served the unchanged repository at http://127.0.0.1:4173. Browser inspection used Chromium 153.0.8010.48. Startup required no dependency installation. For a later manual run from the repository root on this machine: py -3 -m http.server 4173 --bind 127.0.0.1.
